Imagine the scene: you just opened your checking‑account statement and see that the fixed‑income product you bought last month earned less than you expected. Frustration sets in because it feels like your money “stopped” while everyday costs keep rising. That feeling that returns aren’t keeping up with [inflation](/en/glossario/en-inflation) is common, and the root of the problem is often the interbank rate, which acts as a thermometer for most fixed‑income investments.

The **interbank rate** (the average rate banks charge each other for overnight loans) is the benchmark that determines the profitability of many low‑risk investments. When you put your money into a **certificate of deposit (CD)**, an **interbank‑rate fund**, or a **government bond** linked to the interbank rate, the quoted return is expressed as a percentage “of the interbank rate.” In other words, if the interbank rate is 13 % per year and your bond pays 100 % of that rate, you’ll earn roughly 13 % annually. When the interbank rate moves, the return on your investments moves in lockstep.

## How the Interbank Rate Is Calculated  
The interbank rate isn’t set by a government agency; it emerges from daily trades between banks in the clearinghouse. Each ultra‑short‑term loan (usually one day) carries a cost, and a weighted average of those costs forms the interbank rate. Financial market data providers publish it every day, and it’s used as the benchmark for virtually every fixed‑income contract out there.

## Why the Interbank Rate Matters to Everyday Investors  
- **Return reference:** Most fixed‑income products advertise their earnings as “X % of the interbank rate.” If you don’t know what the interbank rate means, you might think you’re earning “X % per year” without realizing that X is tied to a number that can change.  
- **Product comparison:** When two funds both claim to deliver 110 % of the interbank rate, they should, in theory, give you the same return, regardless of the underlying assets.  
- **Inflation impact:** If the interbank rate falls below the consumer price index, your purchasing power shrinks even though the investment looks “profitable.” Tracking the rate helps you decide whether to stay the course or adjust your strategy.

## How the Interbank Rate Affects Different Investment Types  

| Investment Type | How the Interbank Rate Comes In |
|-----------------|---------------------------------|
| **Certificates of Deposit (CDs)** | Pay a percentage of the interbank rate (e.g., 95 % of the rate). When the rate rises, your [yield](/en/glossario/en-yield) rises proportionally. |
| **Interbank‑rate funds** | Invest almost exclusively in securities linked to the interbank rate, so they track the rate almost in real time. |
| **Government Bonds (linked to the central bank base rate)** | Their return follows the central bank base rate, which usually moves hand‑in‑hand with the interbank rate; the gap is typically tiny. |
| **Tax‑Free CDs** | Some offer “X % of the interbank rate” and are exempt from [income tax](/en/glossario/en-income-tax), making the comparison even more attractive. |

## Quick Simulation: How a Change in the Interbank Rate Alters Your Gain  
Suppose you invested an amount equivalent to a **$2,000** monthly salary in a CD that pays 100 % of the interbank rate. If the rate is 12 % per year, your return will be about 12 % at the end of the period. If the rate drops to 9 %, the same CD will only earn 9 %. That difference could cover a daily coffee or reduce a fixed monthly expense, depending on your budget.

## Risks and Cautions When Using the Interbank Rate as a Reference  
- **Rate [volatility](/en/glossario/en-volatility):** While less volatile than [stocks](/en/glossario/en-stocks), the interbank rate can swing noticeably throughout the year, especially during tight monetary policy cycles.  
- **Management fees:** Funds promising “above the interbank rate” may charge management fees that eat into the extra return. Always check the total cost.  
- **[Liquidity](/en/glossario/en-liquidity):** Some rate‑linked products have a lock‑up period. If you need to cash out early, you might lose part of the accrued [interest](/en/glossario/en-interest).  
- **Inflation:** When inflation outpaces the interbank rate, even a “profitable” investment can result in a real loss of purchasing power.

## Practical Strategies to Deal With Interbank Rate Fluctuations  
1. **Diversify across different percentages of the rate:** Combine a CD that pays 95 % of the interbank rate with another that pays 105 % to balance risk and return.  
2. **Add tax‑free products:** Tax‑free CDs that track the rate can boost your net earnings.  
3. **Watch monetary‑policy news:** Central‑bank decisions directly affect the interbank rate; staying informed helps you anticipate moves.  
4. **Set short‑term goals:** When the rate is high, it can be smart to “lock” part of your money in a short‑term instrument to capture the higher yield.

## Frequently Asked Questions  

### What happens if the interbank rate falls below inflation?  
If the rate drops beneath the consumer price index, your investment loses purchasing power even though it shows a positive nominal return. Look for inflation‑protected options, such as bonds linked to the consumer price index.  

### How can I tell if a fund truly outperforms the interbank rate?  
Compare the fund’s net return history with the interbank rate over the same period, and watch out for management fees that can shave off the extra gain.  

### Can the interbank rate ever be negative?  
In extremely expansionary monetary environments, the central‑bank base rate can approach zero, but the interbank rate rarely turns negative. Still, it’s wise to monitor the broader economic context.  

### Can I use the interbank rate as a benchmark for overseas investments?  
The interbank rate is specific to the local market, but for assets held in foreign currencies, equivalents like LIBOR or Euribor serve a similar purpose. FinMoovi’s multi‑currency feature lets you track both benchmarks side by side.

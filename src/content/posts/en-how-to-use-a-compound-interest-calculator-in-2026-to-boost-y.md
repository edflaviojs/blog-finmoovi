## Understanding Compound Interest

Compound interest is basically the “snowball effect” of investing. Unlike [simple interest](/en/glossario/en-simple-interest), which only pays you on the original amount, compound interest adds not just the initial contribution but also the interest that’s already been earned. Think of it like a plant: every time you water it, the plant not only sprouts new leaves, but the existing leaves also help capture more sunlight.

In practice, the classic formula (P × (1 + i)ⁿ) can look intimidating, but what really matters are three variables:

1. **Initial amount (principal)** – the money you already have saved.  
2. **Interest rate** – the percentage your investment earns over the period (could be the return on a certificate of deposit, a mutual fund, or even a dollar‑denominated asset).  
3. **Time** – how many periods (months or years) you’ll let the money stay invested.

When you plug these three numbers into FinMoovi’s calculator, the app spits out the future balance, automatically factoring in any monthly contributions you choose to add along the way.

## Why Most People Underestimate the Power of Compound Interest

- **No visual reference**: Without a projected growth chart, it’s easy to think “that’s too little” and skip the investment.  
- **Hidden fees**: The advertised rate (for example, a fund’s return) often comes with management fees that shave off the real gain.  
- **Irregular contributions**: Investing sporadically kills the compounding effect.

## When Compound Interest Might Not Be the Best Choice

Not every investment yields the same return. If the interest rate is very low (for example, below [inflation](/en/glossario/en-inflation)) or the costs are high (management fees, taxes), the compounding effect can be almost negligible. In those cases, it may be smarter to look at variable‑income options or assets that generate passive income (like real‑estate investment trusts).

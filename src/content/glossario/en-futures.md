## What are futures?

Imagine you’re planning a vacation and you notice that the price of airline tickets is soaring. You wish you could lock in today’s lower price for a flight you’ll take in six months. In the financial world, a similar tool exists: **futures**. A future is a contract that obligates you to buy or sell an asset—like a commodity, a stock index, or a currency—at a predetermined price on a specific future date. It’s like a promise you make today about a transaction that will happen later.

![What are futures?](/images/glossario/futuros-inline-1.webp)



- **Standardized**: Traded on organized exchanges, so the terms (quantity, quality, delivery date) are fixed.
- **Leverage**: You only need to put down a fraction of the contract’s total value, called margin.
- **Settlement**: Can be physical (actual delivery of the asset) or cash‑settled (difference paid in money).

## Why futures matter for you

Most people think futures are only for big traders, but they affect everyday life more than you realize. If you buy a cup of coffee for the price of a daily coffee, the coffee beans’ price might be influenced by futures contracts on coffee. When oil prices jump because of futures trading, the cost of gasoline and the price of a grocery trip can rise too. Understanding futures helps you see why prices move and how you can protect yourself.

- **Hedging**: Farmers lock in a price for their harvest, and airlines lock in fuel costs, keeping prices stable for you.
- **Speculation**: Traders bet on price movements, adding liquidity but also volatility.
- **Price discovery**: Futures markets reveal what the market expects future prices to be, guiding businesses and consumers.

## How futures work in everyday life

Let’s break it down with a simple story. Maria wants to buy a new laptop in three months. She’s worried the price might go up because the components are tied to metal futures (copper, aluminum). She could use a futures contract on those metals to lock in today’s price, indirectly protecting her laptop’s cost. While most individuals don’t trade futures directly, they benefit when companies use them to keep product prices steady.

![How futures work in everyday life](/images/glossario/futuros-inline-3.webp)



**Practical tip:** *If you notice a product you buy regularly is getting more expensive, check if its raw material has an active futures market. That often explains the price swing.*

When you invest through a brokerage, you might see “futures” listed alongside stocks and ETFs. Buying a futures contract means you’re agreeing to settle the price difference at expiration. If the market moves in your favor, you earn; if not, you lose the margin you posted.

## Risks and how to manage them

Futures can be powerful, but they also carry risk, especially because of leverage. A small price move can wipe out your margin quickly. Here’s how to keep the risk in check:

- **Know your exposure**: Only allocate a small portion of your portfolio to leveraged products.
- **Set stop‑loss orders**: Pre‑define a price where you’ll exit the contract to limit losses.
- **Use cash‑settled contracts**: Avoid the hassle of physical delivery if you’re not prepared to handle the actual commodity.

**Practical tip:** *Treat futures like a high‑intensity workout—use them sparingly and always warm up with smaller positions before going all‑in.*

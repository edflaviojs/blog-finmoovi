## Feeling the pinch when the price of a coffee suddenly jumps

You’ve just gone to your favorite café, ordered a latte, and the barista tells you the price went up by the cost of a daily coffee. You wonder why a simple cup of coffee can feel like a luxury overnight. The truth is, the price you pay is linked to something called the **trade balance** – the difference between what a country sells abroad and what it buys from other countries. When the balance tilts toward more imports than exports, the local currency can weaken, and imported goods become pricier. That’s the pain you feel in your wallet, and it’s happening to everyone, not just you.

![Feeling the pinch when the price of a coffee suddenly jumps](/images/glossario/balanca-comercial-inline-1.webp)



## What the trade balance really means

The trade balance (or *balança comercial*) is a simple accounting sheet for a whole country:

- **Exports** – goods and services sold to other nations (the money coming in).  
- **Imports** – goods and services bought from abroad (the money going out).  

If exports > imports, the country has a **trade surplus**; if imports > exports, it runs a **trade deficit**. A surplus can strengthen the local currency, making foreign products cheaper, while a deficit can do the opposite. Think of it as the nation’s “shopping list”: the more you buy from abroad without selling enough of your own stuff, the higher the bill you have to pay.

## Why the trade balance matters for your everyday budget

Even if you never trade goods across borders, the macro picture sneaks into your daily expenses:

![Why the trade balance matters for your everyday budget](/images/glossario/balanca-comercial-inline-3.webp)



- **Imported food and drinks** – coffee beans, chocolate, cheese, etc.  
- **Electronics and gadgets** – smartphones, laptops, gaming consoles.  
- **Travel costs** – flights, hotels, and foreign‑currency purchases.  

When the trade deficit grows, the local currency may lose value, and those imported items cost more. Conversely, a strong export sector can keep the currency stable, protecting your purchasing power. Understanding the trade balance helps you anticipate price shifts before they hit your bank account.

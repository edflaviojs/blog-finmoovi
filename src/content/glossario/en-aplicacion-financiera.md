## What is

The “application” is simply the act of placing your money into a financial instrument so it generates returns. In other words, it’s when you decide not to spend that money now, but let it “work” so that over time it gives you back more than you put in. You don’t need to be an expert; just understand that, like a plant that needs water and light to grow, your money needs a proper “application” to bear fruit.

![What is](/images/glossario/aplicacao-inline-1.webp)

## How it works

When you make an application, you hand your money to an entity (bank, broker or fund) that invests it in different assets (such as debt securities, stocks or real estate). That entity pays interest or [dividends](/es/glossario/es-dividendos), which are the reward for using your capital. The process is summarized in three steps:

- **Deposit** the amount you want to apply in the entity’s account.
- **The entity invests** that money according to the type of application you chose (fixed income, variable, etc.).
- **You receive** the returns periodically (monthly, quarterly, or at maturity).

## Advantages

![Advantages](/images/glossario/aplicacao-inline-3.webp)

- **Passive income generation:** you receive interest without doing anything extra.
- **Protection against inflation:** some instruments adjust their returns to price increases, maintaining your purchasing power.
- **Clear goals:** you can allocate an application to specific targets like buying a car or an emergency fund.
- **Flexibility:** there are short, medium, and long‑term options, depending on your time horizon.

## Risks

Like everything in life, applications are not exempt from risks. It’s important to know them so you don’t get unpleasant surprises:

- **Market risk:** if the application is linked to the stock market, its value can rise and fall quickly.
- **Credit risk:** the entity receiving your money could default on its payments.
- **Limited liquidity:** some [applications](/es/posts/es-5-alternativas-al-mobills-en-2026) only allow withdrawing the money at maturity, which can be a problem if you need cash earlier.
- **Unexpected inflation:** if the return rate is lower than inflation, your money loses purchasing power.

## Practical examples

Imagine your monthly salary is between R$3,000 and R$8,000 and you decide to allocate a part to a fixed‑income application (typically safer). Here are three real scenarios:

- **Case 1:** Salary R$3,500, you apply R$500 monthly in a CDB (Certificate of Deposit) with 110 % of CDI (reference index). In one year, you get approximately R$5,800 in returns, totaling R$11,800 at the end of the period.
- **Case 2:** Salary R$5,200, you apply R$1,000 in a [variable income](/es/glossario/es-renta-variable) investment fund that historically yields 12 % annually. After 2 years, your investment grows to R$2,530, generating R$1,530 in profit.
- **Case 3:** Salary R$7,800, you allocate R$2,000 to a [Tesouro Direto](/es/glossario/es-tesouro-direto) (government bond) with an 8 % annual rate. In 3 years, the accumulated capital reaches R$6,480, representing R$2,480 in interest.

In all cases, the key is to adjust the amount and type of application to your [savings](/es/posts/es-ahorra-en-las-compras-del-supermercado) capacity and your goals.
